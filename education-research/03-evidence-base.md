# The evidence base

What the research literature says about supporting a very bright child in a UK state school.
Includes findings that cut *against* intuition.

## 1. The worry is empirically well-founded

**Ofsted**, on the most able in non-selective state schools:
- **61%** who reached Level 5 in English at KS2 did not get A/A* at GCSE
- **68%** failed to get A*/A in both English and maths
- Over a quarter of high-achieving Y6 pupils **did not make expected progress**
- Mechanism identified: **poor primary→secondary transition** leaves bright children
  under-challenged in KS3

**Sutton Trust, "Missing Talent":**
- **15%** of pupils in the national top 10% at age 11 fall out of the top 25% by GCSE —
  ~**7,000 children a year**
- **Boys are the most likely group to be in it**

## 2. The counterintuitive upside of the state place

**The Big-Fish-Little-Pond Effect (Marsh)** — one of the most replicated findings in
educational psychology. Children of *identical measured ability* have **lower academic
self-concept in academically selective schools** than in non-selective ones.

- Confirmed across **26 countries**; supported by a 2018 meta-analysis
- German cohort data shows the effect **persists 2–4 years after leaving school**

**Why it matters:** academic self-concept feeds motivation, subject choice and aspiration. A
bright child who is a confident big fish in a state primary may carry more academic self-belief
than the same child placed mid-table at a hyper-selective school. This is a real asset of the
choice already made — and an argument for being thoughtful, not maximal, about pushing him into
ever-more-selective environments.

## 3. What works, ranked (Steenbergen-Hu, Makel & Olszewski-Kubilius 2016)

Two second-order meta-analyses, ~100 years of research:

| Intervention | Effect | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Acceleration** (vs same-age peers) | g = 0.70 | Large — strongest single lever |
| Acceleration (overall) | g = 0.42 | Moderate, robust |
| Special grouping for the gifted | g = 0.37 | Works |
| Cross-grade grouping **by subject** | g = 0.26 | Works |
| Within-class grouping | g = 0.19–0.30 | Works |
| **Between-class streaming** | g = 0.04–0.06 | **Doesn't work** |

**Two crucial nuances:**
1. Accelerated pupils *matched* non-accelerated **older** peers (g = 0.09). Acceleration moves a
   child into a harder peer group — it doesn't manufacture ability. The gain is appropriately
   difficult work, not being "ahead".
2. **EEF**: setting/streaming = **0 months' progress**, negative for lower attainers, only "very
   small positive" for high attainers. **Within-class attainment grouping = +3 months**, better
   at primary than secondary.

**Translation:** the wins are *subject-specific* challenge and *within-class* differentiation —
not "top set for everything".

## 4. What his school will do, and why it will resist acceleration

English primary maths is institutionally committed to **teaching for mastery**. The **NCETM
explicitly discourages acceleration** for rapid graspers, arguing it "promotes superficial
understanding". Their model is **greater depth**: same content, richer problems.

So a request to move him up a year in maths will likely be declined — and that's doctrine, not
laziness. The productive ask is:

> **"When he grasps something quickly, what specifically does he move on to?"**

Good answer: rich problem-solving, investigation, generalising.
Bad answer: "he helps the others" or "more of the same questions."

**DfE research** found schools with a clear strategic focus on the most able **all
outperformed**, and that success came from using **fewer strategies well** — specifically
metacognitive, collaborative and peer-learning ones.

## 5. Highest-leverage actions are at home, and cheap

**Reading for pleasure is the standout.** Sullivan & Brown, ~6,000 children in the 1970 British
Cohort Study: reading for pleasure at 10 predicted greater progress by 16 in **vocabulary,
spelling AND mathematics** — and was **more strongly linked to cognitive progress than parental
education**. A child who reads voraciously gains in maths.

**Home learning environment beats parental background.** EPPE study (3,000 children): the
*quality of the home learning environment* is more influential than parental SES or education.
What parents **do** outranks who parents **are**. EEF puts parental engagement at **+5 months**
in early years.

**Metacognition is the best-value school-side intervention** — **+7 months** overall, **+8 at
primary**, among the cheapest available. In practice: "how did you work that out?" and "what
would you try differently?" rather than checking answers.

**Tutoring dosage is counterintuitive.** EEF: 1:1 = **+5 months**, small group = **+4**. But
optimal impact comes from **short regular bursts: ~30 min, 3–5×/week, up to ten weeks**.
Targeted campaigns beat year-round grinding — an argument against a seven-year retainer.

## 6. Motivation, and how a long campaign can backfire

**Praise the process, not the person.** Mueller & Dweck, six studies: children praised for
*intelligence* showed — after failure — less persistence, less enjoyment, worse performance, and
were **more likely to lie about their scores** than children praised for *effort*.

**But be sceptical of the growth-mindset industry.** Sisk et al. (2018), 273 effect sizes,
~365,000 students: correlation of only **r ≈ 0.10** (~1% of variance). Macnamara & Burgoyne
(2023), 122 studies: apparent effects "likely attributable to inadequate study design, reporting
flaws, and bias". *Don't buy mindset products; do praise effort over ability — that specific
finding is narrower and better supported.*

**The "gifted" label carries risk.** A child over-invested in *being identified as* gifted may
avoid challenge to protect the identity — producing exactly the risk-aversion you're trying to
prevent. **Don't call him gifted to his face.**

**Pressure is itself a variable.** Self-determination theory (hundreds of studies): **autonomy,
competence and relatedness** sustain intrinsic motivation; controlling, pressuring involvement
**thwarts** it. Intrinsic motivation predicts engagement, which predicts achievement. For a
parent running a deliberate seven-year plan this is the live hazard — the plan works only if he
experiences it as *his* interests being fed.

## 7. Dose compounds over decades

**Study of Mathematically Precocious Youth (SMPY)** — 5,000+ able children tracked 50 years:

- **"Educational dose" predicts adult outcomes.** Wai et al.: adolescents with a richer density
  of advanced pre-college STEM opportunities produced significantly more **patents, STEM PhDs
  and publications** as adults. Cumulative enrichment shows up decades later.
- **The peer effect is a real benefit.** Enrichment and accelerated classes introduced gifted
  students to "communities of learning" — others like themselves — reducing isolation. A strong
  argument for maths circles on *social* grounds, not just academic.
- Within the top 1%, the **pattern** of ability (mathematical vs verbal vs spatial) shapes
  outcomes as much as the level. Worth knowing his profile, not just his percentile.

## What this changes

1. **Highest-return activity is reading for pleasure.** Free; beat parental education in the data.
2. **Ask "what does he move on to?"** rather than requesting acceleration.
3. **Push for subject-specific challenge and within-class differentiation**, not "top set".
4. **Run tutoring as short targeted campaigns**, not a seven-year retainer.
5. **Watch the motivation cost.** BFLPE says his state-school confidence is an asset; SDT says
   pressure erodes the intrinsic interest that drives long-run achievement.
6. **Don't call him gifted to his face.** Praise the work.
